Secondhand AI
Ambient AI scribes now document millions of patient visits a year [ 1]. They listen to the
consultation and draft a clinical note. A treating clinician may read the note, sign it, and enter
it into the medical record. After that, AI’s role is invisible. A different clinician who later opens
the patient’s chart may read the note as the judgment of her colleague. She has no way to tell
whether the treating clinician wrote it carefully or approved an AI draft with minimal changes.
A patient who reads a follow-up note through her portal sees her doctor’s name; any involvement
of an AI scribe is typically unclear. In one instance, a scribe invented a medication that the
patient had not been prescribed [2]. Neither the downstream clinician nor the patient used AI,
yet both are affected by it. Both judge the note by how it reads in a world where AI has changed
what fluency means.
We call this secondhand AI: the downstream effect of one person’s AI use on what others
receive, how they interpret it, and what they pass on. A student learns from a classmate who
consulted ChatGPT. A manager receives more AI-generated work from her analysts than she
can meaningfully review. In each case, the person affected by AI may not have used it, and may
not even know that it was involved.
Secondhand AI, however, can also benefit the recipient. A parent who uses AI to understand
a child’s diagnosis may ask better questions at the next appointment. A student who uses AI
to find gaps in their reasoning may become a better tutor. The risks of secondhand AI are less
visible and understudied, but they urgently need attention. While much research focuses on
Alice (the person who uses AI), we ask what happens to Bob (the person who receives what she
produces). We begin by briefly discussing how AI changes Alice. We then identify how Alice’s
AI use reaches Bob, and propose how to measure and design for those on the receiving end.
AI changes the sender
AI can help Alice perform better in the moment yet risk keeping her from learning deeply. For
example, high school mathematics students given access to standard GPT-4 performed 48%
better during AI-assisted practice but scored 17% worse on subsequent unassisted exams [3].
Students used the AI assistant as a “crutch,” leaning on it instead of doing the work required
to acquire the skill. As performance was high when AI was used, students had little reason
to suspect their understanding was shallow. Messeri and Crockett [4] call this an illusion of
understanding: Alice believes she understands more than she does because AI filled gaps she
would otherwise have had to confront. The illusion is a first-order problem for Alice. It becomes
a secondhand AI problem the moment she explains what she learned to Bob.
What reaches Bob depends partly on how carefully Alice checks her own work. A survey
found that knowledge workers who expressed higher confidence in generative AI were less likely
to critically evaluate its outputs before passing them on [ 5]. At the same time, AI raises the floor
of quality. A generative AI assistant increased productivity of customer-support workers by 15%
on average, with a 34% improvement for the least experienced workers [6]. As AI narrows the
gap between novices and experts, Bob can no longer use fluency to gauge how much Alice knows.

Three modes of secondhand AI
Alice’s AI use can reach Bob through the content she produces or through who she becomes.
The former creates problems of volume and signal quality, while the latter is harder to see. We
describe three modes below, following the ambient AI scribe through each.
Figure 1: Three modes by which Alice’s use of AI can reach Bob: (1) content overload when Bob
receives material from Alice, (2) illusory expertise present in Alice’s outputs, and (3) an altered sender
(Alice) who is shaped by AI prior to social interactions.
Content overload
Today, AI makes content cheap to produce but costly to verify. The time Alice saves generating
it is time Bob loses reviewing it. Before AI, effort limited output. That limit roughly matched
the capacity of people downstream. AI lowers the cost of content production. The volume of
content that reaches Bob grows while his capacity to review it stays the same unless he uses AI.
AI-generated clinical notes, as of 2026, tend to be longer than physician-authored ones [1].
When an AI scribe produces a note for every visit, the next clinician faces more to read with
no more time to read it. To the extent that a physician-authored note served as a record and
evidence of cognitive engagement with the case, an AI-drafted note weakens that assurance. The
next clinician must choose between verifying the note and seeing the next patient, yet may not
have time for both.
The same dynamic appears in knowledge work. In a survey of 1,150 US desk workers
conducted by researchers at Stanford and BetterUp Labs, 40% reported receiving AI-generated
low-quality work product, or “workslop,” in the previous month [7]. Each instance cost the
recipient nearly two hours to interpret or repair. The authors estimated this verification burden

at $186 per employee per month in lost productivity or more than $9 million per year for a
10,000 person organization. 42% of recipients subsequently viewed the sender as less trustworthy.
When Bob cannot verify what he receives from Alice, he either trusts it uncritically or discounts
everything: neither serves him well. To keep up with Alice’s output, Bob may be pressured to
adopt AI in his review process. Her AI use compels his.
Illusory expertise
AI acts as a mediator between Alice’s knowledge and her output, which can weaken the link
between appearing knowledgeable and being knowledgeable. Before AI, linguistic fluency in a
document carried information. A polished note suggested the clinician had spent time on it.
Those linguistic signals were costly to produce. AI lowers the cost of fluency and can produce
confident prose irrespective of the underlying reasoning. Since Bob judges Alice by what she
produces, he has now lost a reliable cue.
In the case of an ambient scribe, a note in the medical record may appear to reflect authoritative
judgment irrespective of how much time the clinician spent on it. AI may replace the clinician’s
shorthand with fluent prose that simulates professional judgment. Patients rate AI-generated
clinical messages more favorably when they seem polished, but reverse that preference once told
AI was involved [ 8]. This pattern extends beyond medicine. An AI-assisted legal filing may read
as competent legal work but may contain fabricated citations. The cost of discovering those
errors falls to opposing counsel and judges who never use AI themselves.
Fluency obscures weak reasoning and makes language more persuasive. For example, Salvi
et al. [9] found that GPT-4 changed people’s views just as much as human debaters. The AI
assistant became more persuasive when it was given sociodemographic information about the
listener. The polish of Alice’s language may mask Bob’s ability to tell how much she understands.
An altered sender
Bob may interact with Alice without realizing that AI shaped her judgment. Jakesch et al.
[10] found that people who used an opinionated AI writing assistant were more likely to report
holding the AI’s view on a subsequent survey. Their opinion shifted during their AI interaction
without them noticing.
A clinician who uses an ambient AI scribe may find that its colors how she frames cases.
When she briefs a colleague, her framing and language may reflect her AI assistant. Beyond
healthcare, a student who learns a concept with an AI assistant or a parent who researched her
child’s diagnosis with a chatbot may carry the influence of AI into their subsequent conversations.
This mode is hardest to address. Disclosure would have helped if there was an artifact to
label. Here, however, AI’s contribution is how Alice thinks and what she recalls and believes.
Neither she nor Bob may not know which of her ideas are her own and which were shaped by
the AI she used earlier.
Measuring and designing for the recipient
Secondhand AI becomes visible only when researchers study what happens between people.
Current evaluation frameworks tend to measure first-order outcomes: task completion, satisfaction,
persistence or memory. They do not measure what happens to Bob and all the others with whom
Alice interacts. Three measurement mechanisms can fill this gap. Dyadic experiments randomly
assign Alice to use AI or not, then measure what Bob learns from her output. Outcomes should
include Bob’s accuracy, confidence, trust, verification effort, and ability to transmit the knowledge
onward. Transmission-chain experiments track how content degrades as it passes from Alice
to Bob, and then to Carol and Dave. Institutional audits can examine workflows: whether

content can be traced back to its source, how much downstream review it requires, and where AI
involvement is lost.
For content overload, the priority is to measure how Bob’s verification behavior shifts as the
amount of AI-generated content grows. The workslop survey used self-reports to establish that
the burden of review is real [7]. Controlled experiments could vary the proportion of AI-generated
content in Bob’s workflow to measure whether his scrutiny drops as volume rises and at what
threshold he shifts from checking carefully to blindly trusting AI outputs.
For illusory expertise, dyadic experiments can isolate the secondhand AI effect directly.
Cavalier et al. [8] suggest that disclosing AI involvement is meaningful, but its effect on calibration
is unknown. One way to test this: randomly assign medical students to prepare a case summary
with or without AI assistance. Give each summary to a second student who is not told whether
AI was involved. Measure whether AI-assisted summaries produce higher confidence in the second
student without improving her actual understanding. Then, vary disclosure: does telling the
second student that AI may have been involved restore her calibration?
For an altered Alice, measurement must also focus on human-human interaction. Alice can
learn a concept with or without AI and then teach it to Bob. Measure whether Bob can get the
answer correct, explain his reasoning, and handle edge cases. Does Bob recognize the limits of
what he has been told? Transmission-chain experiments test the limits of understanding as it
passes from link to link: is Bob able to explain a concept to Carol well enough that Dave will
understand too?
Design interventions differ depending on whether Bob is receiving Alice’s output or talking
to a changed Alice. In the scribe case, this could mean flagging sections of a signed clinical
note that the clinician edited heavily versus those approved unchanged, so the next clinician can
calibrate her reading accordingly. Legal marking rules, such as the EU AI Act’s transparency
provisions, are a start because they ask whether AI was involved. Secondhand AI requires more:
enough information for the next person to scrutinize what they receive. If AI use changes Alice,
institutions may move beyond disclosure of use and check whether Alice still has the ability to
exercise independent judgment. In practice, some institutions may ask Alice to demonstrate her
judgment unaided, so that any erosion in her abilities do not affect Bob.
Secondhand AI names the space where one person’s AI assistance becomes another person’s
epistemic environment. Those recipients did not choose to use AI, may not know AI was involved,
and often lack a way to contest or correct the effect. Secondhand smoke is an imperfect analogy,
as the potential benefits of AI exposure should be considered alongside the potential harms. But
the structural parallel is helpful: the costs of AI use do not stay with the user. They travel
through the social channels that carry knowledge, trust, and professional judgment. Whether we
detect secondhand AI and its effects in time is a question our field has barely begun to ask.
